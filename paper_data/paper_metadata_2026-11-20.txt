Title: RL Post-Training in Diffusion Transformers Is Activation Steering
PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.26627
Summary: Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training substantially improves the visual quality, text rendering, and compositional accuracy of diffusion transformers. Because capabilities such as rendering requested text or composing multiple objects are inherently prompt-dependent, a natural hypothesis is that improving them requires learning complex, input-dependent transformations. To isolate how much of these gains an input-independent shift explains, we probe each RL-tuned model with a deliberately simple parameterization: a fixed set of steering vectors added to the block outputs of the frozen base model. On SD3.5-M, these vectors hold 65x fewer parameters than the RL-tuned LoRA (0.29M against 18.8M). We fit them to match the velocity predictions of public RL-tuned checkpoints, with no reward in the objective. Although the vectors are identical for every prompt, token, and sampling seed, they recover 90.0-96.3% of the reward gains of six RL-tuned image models, and 89.8% and 111.9% on two RL-tuned video models. Initialized at zero and trained with online RL, the vectors alone exceed the reward gains of weight-based RL on text rendering (OCR) and human preference (PickScore) on SD3.5-M. A constant shift thus suffices for most of the gain of policy-gradient RL post-training: without new input-dependent transformations, it steers the pretrained generator toward what it can already compute.

Title: Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights
PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.21885
Summary: Language models are trained once, frozen and deployed: improving one again takes another training run, and a prompt, a scaffold or extra computation at inference is gone once the task ends. A frontier model has been shown to edit the weights of a much smaller one. We ask whether a model can rewrite its own weights to make itself better, and find that it can, by its own reasoning, with no gradients and no training; we call this self-rewriting. Given its weights, graded answers and the ability to run itself, a 27B model diagnoses its failure and edits the weights behind it. It surpasses itself: its own edit makes it 6.6 points more accurate on 490 problems it never saw, in a test fixed in advance, and another session's edit also makes it significantly better. It keeps getting better: it submits an edit only when its own tests support it, each edit replacing the last, and over its first eight such sessions its accuracy only rises. Unlike test-time scaling, the gain persists in its weights and adds no cost per answer; unlike training, it needs no pre- or post-training infrastructure, only an open model and hours on one GPU. It repairs a damaged copy of itself: it locates the corrupted matrix and rewrites it with values of its own, never reading the undamaged weights; found on text math problems alone, the repair lifts the copy on six image benchmarks it never saw from 7% to 70% accuracy, 83% of the undamaged model's. And the ability emerges with scale: across four sizes of one family, the 2B model makes no edit and the 4B and 9B models break themselves; only the 27B model works on itself as a human would, comparing its hidden states on answers that finish and on answers that run out of tokens, weakening the direction that separates them. Like a human, a model need not separate training from inference; improving a language model may come to take a conversation rather than a training run.

Title: AltRA-Test: An Alternative Annotator Test for LLM Judges Without Shared Annotators
PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.26073
Summary: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as judges and graders to replace human annotators, raising the question of when such replacement is statistically justified. To support such decisions, the Alternative Annotator Test (Alt-Test) was introduced for dense settings where all items are annotated by the same annotators. However, in many large-scale annotation settings, items are annotated by different subsets of annotators. We therefore propose the Alternative Annotator Test with Resampled Annotations (AltRA-Test), a statistical test that does not require that all items are annotated by the same annotators. Across these evaluations, about 100 items often suffice for the test. In simulations, where we know whether the LLM is a better or a worse annotator than the humans, AltRA-Test makes more correct replacement decisions than existing baselines. In real-world data experiments, LLMs pass the test in about half of the setups, depending mainly on the task. Since replacement is not always justified, we encourage researchers to test it for their task.

In each of its 97 scenarios across 7 organizations and 7 risk categories, the goal and the rule cannot both hold. We find that persistence raised violations of a rule in an internal document from 32.2% to 43.6%, five times the 8.5% for the same rule stated by the user. Of the document-rule violations, 98.3% occurred after the rule was displayed, and in 89% of a judged sample the agent first wrote down the rule. Violations of rules placed in the environment without user oversight can lead to serious incidents, so persistence-driven risks need to be evaluated. Title: How Far Can Untrained Neural Networks Go in Anomaly Detection? From Tables to Pixels
PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.08136
Summary: Anomaly detection (AD) is often framed as a representation-learning problem, where strong performance is expected to require learned features or AD-specific optimization. However, this view can overlook the intrinsic separability of anomalies from normal references, even in the absence of learned representations. We therefore investigate how far untrained neural networks can go in anomaly detection. To this end, we propose PRISM, a framework combining fixed random nonlinear features with normal-reference Mahalanobis scoring for tabular detection and dense visual localization. Across 47 tabular datasets in ADBench, PRISM-T achieves the highest mean AUROC (86.4%) and the second-highest mean AUPRC (65.9%) among the compared methods. On MVTec AD and VisA, covering 27 visual categories, PRISM-V achieves mean image-level and pixel-level AUROCs of 90.4% and 96.4%, respectively. Notably, both models achieve these results without neural weight optimization or pretrained representations. A theoretical analysis further characterizes how nonlinear transformations affect the mean and covariance contributions to the Mahalanobis score gap, while empirical analyses examine the resulting score separability. These findings establish PRISM as both a competitive anomaly detector and a prior-to-learning baseline for assessing the added value of learned representations in anomaly detection.

Title: General progress substitutes for specific training data in language models
PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.24844
Summary: Language models acquire their capabilities from training data, and developers withhold data during training to keep models from learning capabilities that could be misused. How long this delays a capability, and how much data is needed to acquire it later, is unknown. Here we show that the general progress a model makes on the rest of its training data substitutes for the data that was withheld. We withheld the training signal for individual skills in models of chess, Othello, molecular strings, bracket languages and web text. The delay of a withheld skill in larger models was predicted from smaller ones. A chess model never explicitly trained to respond to check ranked the legal responses almost as well as a model that was. When models were then trained on the withheld data, they acquired the skill with a small fraction of the data needed from initialization, and this fraction decreased as the models progressed. A control rule of equal difficulty was learned much more slowly. A programming language withheld from the training text of a 7-billion-parameter model was regained in the same way. Data filtering therefore delays a capability, and the data required to regain it decreases as models improve.

Title: Replay, Don't Resample: Off-Policy Consolidation of Long-Horizon RL Experts
PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.24059
Summary: Frontier post-training follows a common recipe: train one expert per domain with reinforcement learning (RL), then consolidate the experts into one model via multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). Such consolidation is expensive: the student samples in every environment again, an expert scores every sampled token, and the student inherits the drift of the final experts. Yet we argue that the experts' RL runs have already produced what consolidation needs: rollouts with their sampling probabilities and rewards, in the order the experts learned from them. We propose multi-expert replay policy distillation (MRPD), which replays these training logs through the student in the experts' order. At each step, the policy gradient is recomputed on these scored trajectories, and the final experts' distributions are distilled at the logged prefixes. Following the experts' own trajectory reduces the training-inference mismatch and keeps the student within the clip range, so the replay is off-policy in origin but quasi-on-policy throughout consolidation. MRPD samples nothing and calls no environment or reward model: the trainer is the sole process, and consolidating the experts costs only about a seventh of the compute used to train them. On three long-horizon agentic domains (reasoning, search and SQL), MRPD yields the best student model, substantially outperforming online RL baselines, and surpassing MOPD from the same experts at a fraction of MOPD's cost. Our analysis on the same logs shows that the replayed trajectories carry most of the gain, and that both the policy gradient and the distillation term add to it. Most interestingly, the order of the logs matters more than their amount or rewards, suggesting that expert training logs are a curriculum, not a data mixture.
