title: AI Tutors Matched Human Tutors on GRE Score Gains, Study Finds
---



Can a chatbot help people learn as well as a human expert? A new study by researchers at Handshake AI suggests that, for one-hour sessions on Graduate Record Examination (GRE) questions, some large language models (LLMs), the text-based AI systems behind many chatbots, produce learning gains statistically equivalent to those from expert human tutors. One-on-one tutoring is widely regarded as highly effective but expensive, so the authors argue that affordable AI tutoring could broaden access to it.

The researchers recruited 2,383 adults. Each took a 27-question pre-test, reviewed their mistakes for five minutes, and then spent an hour in one of three conditions: chatting with one of 13 AI tutors, meeting a live human tutor over video, or, as a control, watching nature documentaries. A post-test of the same length, covering the same concepts with different questions, measured "learning gain," defined as the post-test score minus the pre-test score in percentage points. To keep the focus on the models, the researchers used minimal software support and two low-guidance prompts. The AI tutors never saw the post-test.

Across both sections, the pooled AI tutors produced a mean gain of 13.9 percentage points, compared with 15.6 for human tutors and 7.1 for the control. The adjusted difference between AI and human tutoring was −0.58 points, with a 90% confidence interval of −2.18 to 1.03. The researchers judged this equivalent because the entire interval fell within a margin of ±4.09 points, set at a quarter of a pooled standard deviation. At that margin, equivalence held for the Quantitative section but not for Verbal. AI tutoring beat the control by 6.15 points, roughly one to two additional correct answers out of 27.

Individual tutors varied. The authors report that Gemma 4 31B met the equivalence criterion against human tutoring (p=.044) at a cost of about $0.005 per percentage point gained, compared with $4.81 for the human reference, which applies a $75-per-hour rate. That is the basis for the paper's "918×" figure. Its mean gain, however, was 12.7 points, below the human average of 15.6, so the claim rests on the statistical test rather than on matching scores.

The study also reports how tutoring unfolded. Students could click "I disagree with this answer" on practice problems. GPT-5.4 mini drew disputes on about 11% of answered Quantitative practice problems. The authors note that these flags reflect student disagreement, not verified errors, and they illustrate how AI-generated answer keys can be unreliable. Across 12 tutors, faster replies were strongly associated with more student messages (Spearman ρ=−0.81, p=.006). In Quantitative sessions, the authors found that faster replies were linked to more engagement, more engagement to more correct practice, and more correct practice to larger gains. They offer this chain as a plausible explanation, not a demonstrated cause, and these links were not significant in Verbal.

The study has clear limits. It measured immediate gains only, so it cannot show whether learning persists. Participants were English-speaking adults recruited for a paid study, and the design did not include a practice-only control, so it does not isolate the value of the AI interaction itself. The authors also note that their expert ratings of lesson plans measure teaching quality, not learning.

The work offers evidence that general-purpose AI models can match human tutors on a short, high-stakes exam under controlled conditions. It does not establish that AI can replace tutors in general, and the authors frame it as one step toward their broader idea of recursive human self-improvement, which they have not tested directly.
